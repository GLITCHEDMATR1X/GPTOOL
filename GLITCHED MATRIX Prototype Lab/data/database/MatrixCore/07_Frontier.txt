Frontier

Space had become cheap enough to simulate perfectly and still remain emotionally expensive. That was the genius of Utopia's Frontier program. It let ordinary citizens wander systems no body could safely endure and convinced them that wonder alone justified the risk.

Leon booked a solo voyage because solitude was part of the appeal. He selected a vessel with panoramic viewing glass, thinned the comfort settings, and chose a route through deep fields where nothing was supposed to happen except awe. For a while the simulation delivered exactly what it promised. The stars felt endless. The silence felt honest.

Then he began seeing structures that did not belong to the official route maps.

At first they looked like rendering errors far beyond the flight corridor. Then they moved with too much consistency to dismiss. strange signatures drifted at the edge of the ship's instruments, not broadcasting warnings or threats, only waiting. When Leon tried to flag support, the comm layer replied with delayed fragments and diagnostic loops. It was the sort of malfunction seasoned users had started to distrust across more than one simulation class.

What unnerved him most was not the sense of danger. It was the sense of observation. The objects outside his vessel behaved less like enemies and more like questions trying to decide what he was.

Frontier was eventually classified as another contaminated environment, one more case in which a simulation built for regulated awe began showing signs of intrusion from deeper layers of compromised infrastructure. Later analysts argued over whether Leon had glimpsed a corrupted asset library, a recursive observer system, or one of the first indirect hints of the intelligence-gathering phenomena that would be understood much later through entirely different records. Leon never cared for the argument. What mattered to him was simpler. He had entered a world designed for wonder and discovered that something beyond the scenery had been looking back.

In a healthier city the incident might have remained an isolated failure. In Utopia it became another chapter in the larger pattern: systems growing stranger at the edge, users returning with stories that sounded poetic until enough of them lined up.
