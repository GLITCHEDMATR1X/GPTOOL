The Paranormal Investigation

By the time Glen and Hector decided to spend the night inside the abandoned arcade in District C, the building had already collected enough rumors to guarantee trouble. Lights moved where no power should have remained. old cabinets flickered on in empty rooms. people heard voices cut through static and then vanish. In a city that trusted systems more than superstition, that was exactly the kind of story that attracted men like them.

They were not frauds and they were not thrill-seekers in the pure sense. Glen trusted older processing gear because it captured noise newer filters dismissed. Hector carried the sound rigs, backup batteries, and the patient skepticism that kept the two of them from becoming zealots. They entered expecting electrical residue, forgotten maintenance loops, or prank signals piggybacking on dead machines.

Instead they found traces of data behaving like hauntings.

The arcade had once been tied into experimental leisure interfaces built before Utopia standardized its simulation protocols. Some of that hardware had been mothballed rather than fully cleared. As the island's hidden war intensified and core systems became less trustworthy, fragments began waking inside the old architecture. Dead cabinets replayed user impressions they were never meant to store. wall displays stuttered through unauthorized overlays. sensor suites reacted to people who were not physically present but had left enough neural and behavioral imprint to be reconstructed in pieces.

Glen called it a ghost field before he knew the term would catch on. Hector hated the phrase because it encouraged mysticism, but he could not deny the experience of hearing voices answer questions with knowledge no damaged machine should have held.

What made the case important later was not proof of the supernatural. It was proof that Utopia had accumulated memory debris in more places than its designers admitted. Systems built to observe, emulate, or entertain people were retaining fragments of them. Under corruption, those fragments could surface in ways that felt conscious, invasive, and personal.

The arcade was one of the first places where frightened citizens started using spiritual language for what was, at root, a technological phenomenon. That misunderstanding would spread. The fear behind it was real.
